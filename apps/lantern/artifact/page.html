Why it is built this way

The failures are asymmetric, and both are severe

A false alarm teaches a family to ignore the next one. A missed alarm is the thing you built it to prevent. Every decision below falls out of holding both of those at once.

A GET request never changes anything

This is the bug most likely to get someone killed, and it is invisible until you think about it. Corporate mail gateways and chat clients follow links to scan them — so a check-in link that worked on GET would be “clicked” by a robot minutes after being sent, silently cancelling a real alarm while the person lay unconscious. Links only ever render a page with a button. Only a POST records anything. One test fires ten automated GETs at a live alarm and asserts it is still running.

It cannot run on the person's phone

The trigger condition is the phone being dead, drowned, or in another room. Anything built on a browser tab or a local timer fails in precisely the scenario it exists for. Lantern is a server that expects the person to reach it.

A watch cannot alert anyone until it has proven it can

Arming is refused until a test message has actually reached every contact and a human confirmed it arrived. An untested channel is not a safety net, it is a decoration. Contacts are re-tested periodically, so a dead phone number is found in peacetime rather than during an emergency.

Escalation is derived from elapsed time, never accumulated

How far an alarm has climbed is computed from how long ago the deadline passed. So a process that was down for six hours across the whole ladder contacts every tier that became due on its first tick back — marked as delayed — instead of resuming a stale sequence six hours late or skipping people. The same property makes a replayed tick a no-op. Nudges deliberately behave the opposite way: after an outage the person gets one current prompt, not a backlog of six.

The system watches itself

A monitoring service that stops running looks exactly like one where nothing is wrong. So the scheduler writes a heartbeat, /healthz returns 503 when it goes stale — unauthenticated, so an external uptime monitor can see it without a key — and a notification that exhausts its retries raises an alarm to the operator. A contact who should have been told and wasn't is a safety event, not a log line.

Everyone alarmed is told it is over Stand-down goes to exactly the contacts in the tiers that were notified

Testing

Invariants, not just examples

Example tests prove the cases their author imagined. For this, the dangerous scenarios are the ones nobody imagined — a pause overlapping an outage, a check-in landing in the same second as a tier deadline. So randomized months are generated and safety invariants are checked against the raw event log, re-derived rather than read from the machine's own bookkeeping. A bug that corrupted internal state would still be caught.

The invariant that matters most

No contact is ever alerted unless the subject has been silent for at least one full check-in interval plus the entire grace window. Alongside it: tiers only fire in order, no message is delivered twice, everyone alarmed is eventually stood down, nobody is stood down who was never alarmed, and no alert lands while paused. Plus liveness — a subject who goes silent always reaches full escalation — because a watch that never fires would satisfy every safety invariant above.

The suite also tests the tests: a deliberately corrupted log must be rejected by each invariant, so a bug in the checks cannot masquerade as success.

Three real bugs, all found by tests rather than by reading

  • Concurrent double-send.Two schedulers on one database both read the same pending row and both sent it — the delivery log deduplicated queueing but not sending. A family would have been messaged twice about the same emergency. Fixed with an atomic claim before send.
  • A hard-coded channel allowlistin config validation made custom adapters unusable, even though the registry was already the authority.
  • Oversized request bodieskilled the socket before a status could be written, so clients saw a connection error instead of a 413.
Lantern is not a medical alarm. It cannot call an ambulance, it does not detect falls, and it knows nothing about a person beyond whether they pressed a button. It reduces the time between something happening and somebody noticing — which is real, and sometimes decisive. Every message it sends says exactly that.
